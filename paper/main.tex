\title{Borrowing from other patients and from the patient's own past, not from healthy brains: pre-registered calibration transfer for motor-imagery brain--computer interfaces after stroke}

\begin{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Objective.} Motor-imagery brain--computer interfaces (BCIs) for stroke rehabilitation must be calibrated on each patient, and the few trials a patient can give leave decoders unreliable. We tested which borrowed data shortens calibration after stroke: healthy recordings, a physiology-based simulation of stroke EEG, an EEG foundation model, other patients' recordings, or the patient's own earlier sessions.
\textbf{Approach.} We harmonised three public stroke cohorts and one healthy cohort: 50 acute patients imagining left- versus right-hand movement, 24 chronic patients imagining moving the affected hand versus resting, 27 patients imagining walking versus idling at three time points, and 109 healthy participants. Confirmatory hypotheses were committed to a version-controlled log before a locked test set or the new cohort was loaded, and each was run once. Per-patient Riemannian decoders were compared with deep transfer, a frozen foundation model, LesionSim (healthy EEG edited to mimic weaker, shifted and more variable lesioned-side activity) and pooling other patients' data into a re-centred Riemannian decoder.
\textbf{Main results.} With five calibration pairs, pooling other patients raised accuracy on locked test patients from 77.1\pct{} to 81.4\pct{} (one-sided $p = 0.047$) and, in a pre-registered replication on the third cohort, from 69.1\pct{} to 70.8\pct{} ($p = 0.019$). Stacking the real-data sources with online re-centring, co-adaptive updating and online model averaging raised accuracy on eight never-used patients from 77.1\pct{} to 88.7\pct{} in the first session and from 75.9\pct{} to 88.1\pct{} in later sessions; every patient improved (both $p = 0.004$). The stack beat co-adaptive recalibration of the patient's own decoder by 5.2 points ($p = 0.020$), and by 3.0 points ($p = 0.047$) once updates were made only between rest--imagery pairs, which keeps the alternating trial order out of the decoder. All six confirmatory verdicts held under that audit and on ICA-cleaned recordings, and every decoder fell to chance with shuffled labels. Without labels after calibration, returning patients still gained 11.9 points. At a second hospital (49 acute patients, left- versus right-hand imagery), the stack beat the own decoder by 4.4 points ($p = 0.003$) but not co-adaptive recalibration. Splitting the pooled decoder's band into mu, low-beta and high-beta covariances added 2.3 points (Holm $p = 0.021$). A returning patient's earlier sessions added 3.2 points in later sessions (Holm $p = 0.0045$), and a decoder built from them with no new labels was non-inferior to five labelled calibration pairs. Healthy data helped in no form, LesionSim closed none of the gap to real patients, and the foundation-model probe trailed by 14--24 points. Imagery-versus-rest decoding also drew on eye movements and posterior alpha; on the dataset authors' ICA-cleaned recordings the band-bank pooled decoder kept its gain, while the single-band gain shrank.
\textbf{Significance.} Few-trial stroke BCIs should borrow from real patients and from the patient's own history, not from simulated or healthy brains, and combining these sources with adaptation during the session brings few-trial decoding close to 90\pct{}. Code, a real-time implementation and the pre-registration trail are released.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Brain--computer interfaces that turn movement-related brain activity into feedback can improve upper-limb recovery after stroke. A meta-analysis of nine randomised controlled trials with 235 patients found a standardised mean difference of 0.79 in upper-limb Fugl-Meyer scores in favour of BCI-based therapy \citep{cervera2018}. Controlled studies that coupled sensorimotor desynchronisation to hand and arm orthoses \citep{ramosmurguialday2013} or to functional electrical stimulation \citep{biasiucci2018} reported clinically meaningful gains, lasting 6--12 months in the latter. Such therapy depends on a decoder that reliably detects the patient's attempt to move. Motor-imagery decoders are trained per user, and even healthy users vary widely: across 54 participants the mean accuracy was 71.1\pct{}, and 53.7\pct{} were classed as unable to use motor imagery proficiently \citep{lee2019}. Stroke patients can reach similar levels when a decoder is trained on their own data \citep{ang2011}. However, each calibration trial costs effort that patients early after stroke can rarely give, and the information the decoder needs may sit in the contralesional rather than the ipsilesional hemisphere \citep{mansour2022}.

The usual remedy is to borrow data. Deep networks pretrained on healthy participants and fine-tuned on a patient detected motor imagery against rest in 71 stroke patients at 71.15\pct{} \citep{nagarajan2024}. Applied to the public cohort of 50 acute patients we also study \citep{liu2024}, healthy-to-stroke transfer of a reinforcement-learning decoder raised the mean first-epoch success rate only from 49.46\pct{} to 51.82\pct{}, and 21 of the 50 patients showed negative transfer \citep{via2026}. EEG foundation models transferred zero-shot from healthy participants to stroke patients performed at chance \citep{nguyen2026}. Generative augmentation has been used to synthesise a patient's motor-imagery features from that patient's own resting EEG \citep{xu2026}. What has not been tested is which \emph{source} of borrowed data helps a new stroke patient most when calibration is short: healthy recordings, simulated stroke recordings, generic pretrained representations, or other patients. Rehabilitation also runs over many sessions, so a second question matters as much: how far a returning patient's own earlier recordings can replace calibration.

Small-sample EEG studies are also prone to optimistic evaluation. Splitting segments of one participant across training and test sets inflates accuracy \citep{brookshire2024}, cross-validation on a few dozen trials has wide error bars \citep{varoquaux2018}, and theoretical chance levels are too lenient for small test sets \citep{combrisson2015}. We therefore built the evaluation before the methods: patient-level locked test sets, a fresh replication cohort, chronological calibration, blocked cross-validation, and pre-registered confirmatory hypotheses that were committed to a version-controlled log before the locked patients or the new cohort were loaded.

This study makes the following contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A leakage-safe benchmark of three public stroke cohorts and one healthy cohort, harmonised to a common montage and lesion side, with locked test sets, a replication cohort and a timestamped pre-registration trail (Section~\ref{sec:design}).
  \item A head-to-head comparison of four sources of borrowed data for few-trial calibration: healthy recordings (deep transfer and Riemannian pooling), a physiology-based stroke simulator (LesionSim) in six variants, a frozen EEG foundation model, and other patients' recordings (Sections~\ref{sec:s3}--\ref{sec:lesionsim}).
  \item A confirmed and replicated result: pooling other patients into a re-centred Riemannian decoder shortens calibration, on a locked test set and on a second cohort with a different limb and trial design (Sections~\ref{sec:s3} and~\ref{sec:s4}).
  \item Two further confirmed improvements: a mu/low-beta/high-beta band bank in the pooled decoder, and calibration from a returning patient's own earlier sessions, which with no new labels matched five labelled calibration pairs (Section~\ref{sec:s4}).
  \item A combined decoder that stacks these sources with online re-centring, co-adaptive updating and online model averaging. On eight never-used patients it raised accuracy by about 12 points in their first and later sessions, helping every patient, and beat co-adaptive recalibration of the patient's own decoder by 3 points; without labels after calibration, returning patients still gained 12 points. The result survived audits of trial order, shuffled labels and shared patients, and at a second hospital the stack beat the own decoder but not co-adaptive recalibration (Section~\ref{sec:combo}).
  \item An exploratory sweep of 40 decoder variants, and artifact controls showing that imagery-versus-rest decoding also draws on eye movements and posterior alpha, with a sensitivity analysis on ICA-cleaned recordings (Sections~\ref{sec:sweep} and~\ref{sec:artifacts}).
  \item SafeTransfer, a calibration-only gate that chooses between a patient's own decoder and a transferred one, or abstains (Section~\ref{sec:gate}).
  \item Quantified evaluation pitfalls on these data, including a numerical failure of Euclidean alignment that produced a spurious negative-transfer finding, and floating-point ties that understated a confirmatory $p$-value (Section~\ref{sec:pitfalls}).
  \item A real-time implementation over the Lab Streaming Layer, verified to reproduce the offline results (Section~\ref{sec:online}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Cohorts}

Table~\ref{tab:cohorts} lists the cohorts. \textbf{S1} \citep{liu2024} contains 50 acute stroke patients (1--30 days after onset) who imagined left- or right-hand grasping in 40 alternating trials (2\,s instruction, 4\,s imagery, 2\,s break), recorded with 29 EEG and 2 EOG channels at 500\,Hz. \textbf{S3} \citep{wang2026} contains 24 chronic stroke patients (3--14 months) recorded with 59 EEG channels at 250\,Hz at three time points; each run interleaves rest, affected-hand imagery and imagery of a supernumerary sixth finger. Task~B pairs every affected-hand imagery epoch with the rest epoch immediately before it, giving 40 pairs per patient at the first time point. \textbf{S4} \citep{liu2025gait} contains 27 stroke patients (1--12 months after onset; 18 with right and 9 with left hemiplegia) who imagined walking or stayed idle in 10 + 10 trials per run, in random order, recorded with 60 EEG channels at three time points (pre-treatment, post-treatment and follow-up). It served only as a replication cohort and is described with the confirmatory analyses (Section~\ref{sec:design}). S3 and S4 were recorded at the same institution with the same amplifier family; ages, sexes and paralysed sides suggest that some patients may appear in both, although no S3 data enter any S4 analysis. \textbf{H1}, the PhysioNet motor movement/imagery database \citep{schalk2004,goldberger2000}, provides 109 healthy participants. A fourth cohort, HEFMI-ICH \citep{shi2025}, was screened but excluded from decoding: its channel order and labels were verified against the stored metadata and eyes-closed alpha topography, yet the motor-band power of its healthy controls varied by only about 3\pct{} across the whole trial, too little imagery-related desynchronisation to decode.

\subsection{Harmonisation}

All recordings were band-pass filtered at 1--40\,Hz, cut 0.5--4.0\,s after the imagery cue (or the rest cue), resampled to 128\,Hz (448 samples), and reduced to a 14-channel motor template (F3, Fz, F4, FC3, FCz, FC4, C3, Cz, C4, CP3, CP4, P3, Pz, P4) re-referenced to its common average. Patients with left hemiplegia were mirrored across the midline so that every lesion lies in the left hemisphere; in S1 the hand labels were swapped accordingly, so ``right hand'' always denotes the affected hand. Healthy participants were assigned a virtual left-hemisphere lesion. Pipelines for harmonising motor-imagery datasets exist for healthy cohorts \citep{liu2025cleanmi}; lesion-side normalisation extends harmonisation to stroke. Data were loaded with MNE-Python \citep{gramfort2013} and MOABB \citep{jayaram2018}; the original S1 clinical table was used because a widely mirrored copy lacks the clinical columns. S4 was read from its BIDS re-release, whose events mark the onsets of gait imagery and idling; its paralysed sides came from the original release's participant table.

\subsection{Evaluation design}
\label{sec:design}

\paragraph{Locked test sets.} Before any method was tuned on a cohort, a stratified random subset of its patients was locked away (S1: 15 of 50, stratified by paralysed side and NIHSS above the median; S3: 8 of 24, stratified by paralysed side and time since stroke above the median). Locked patients were loaded only to test a hypothesis that had been written down and committed beforehand (Table~\ref{tab:prereg}). S4 served as a replication cohort: its four hypotheses were committed while its download was still running, amended before any of its recordings was decoded, and each was tested once on all patients.

\paragraph{Protocols.} Two within-patient protocols were used. \emph{Blocked cross-validation} (S1 development) splits a patient's 40 trials into four contiguous blocks of ten and tests each block with a model trained on the other three, so that slow drifts cannot leak between training and test trials. \emph{Chronological calibration} (S1 screening, all of S3 and S4) calibrates on the first $k$ trials (or rest--imagery pairs) of each class in recording order and tests on all remaining trials, as a clinical session would. Every reference statistic, filter, re-centring matrix and model was fitted on training or calibration trials only.

\paragraph{Statistics.} Comparisons are paired across patients: Wilcoxon signed-rank tests on per-patient accuracy (zero differences dropped), Holm correction within each family of comparisons \citep{holm1979}, and bootstrap 95\pct{} confidence intervals of the mean. Accuracy differences were rounded to $10^{-9}$ before ranking, so that differences equal in exact arithmetic are treated as ties (Section~\ref{sec:pitfalls}). The S4 hypotheses were tested in a fixed sequence: the pooling replication first at $\alpha = 0.05$, and only if it held, the other three with Holm correction. Non-inferiority used a margin of 2 points, the pre-registered threshold for harm. A patient counts as harmed when a method scores at least 2 points below the patient's own decoder. Per-patient significance uses the exact one-sided binomial test \citep{combrisson2015}; with 39 test trials an accuracy of at least 66.7\pct{} is required.

\paragraph{Pre-registration.} Hypotheses, the frozen pipeline and the test statistic were committed to the project's results log before the locked patients were loaded; the commit timestamps are listed in Table~\ref{tab:prereg}. Each confirmatory analysis was run once, and its outcome was reported whatever it was.

\subsection{Decoders}

\paragraph{Own decoder.} Per patient, trials were band-pass filtered at 8--30\,Hz, and spatial covariance matrices were estimated with oracle approximating shrinkage \citep{chen2010}, re-centred on the Riemannian mean of the training trials \citep{zanini2018}, mapped to the tangent space \citep{barachant2012} and classified by L2-regularised logistic regression ($C = 1$). Implementations used pyRiemann \citep{pyriemann}.

\paragraph{Window ensemble (ens16).} Sixteen own decoders, one per combination of four windows (0.5--4, 0.5--2.5 and 2--4\,s after the imagery cue, and 4--6\,s, the post-imagery period) and four bands (mu 8--13\,Hz, beta 13--30\,Hz, 8--30\,Hz, and a filter bank of all three), each fitted on the training trials; their predicted probabilities were averaged.

\paragraph{Pooled decoder.} Calibration epochs of the target patient were pooled with donor epochs. Each domain---every donor, and the target's calibration set---was re-centred on its own Riemannian mean, which places all of them at the identity \citep{zanini2018}; tangent vectors were then taken at the identity and one logistic regression was fitted on all of them. All donor epochs together received the same total weight as the calibration epochs. Donors were the other development patients, healthy participants, or simulated stroke data; in S4 they were the other 26 patients.

\paragraph{Band-bank pooled decoder.} The same pooled decoder, but with covariance matrices estimated separately in the mu (8--13\,Hz), low-beta (13--20\,Hz) and high-beta (20--30\,Hz) bands; each band is re-centred per domain and the three tangent vectors are placed side by side before the logistic regression. The theta band, which the best development variant also contained, was left out because eye movements separate rest from imagery there (Section~\ref{sec:artifacts}).

\paragraph{Earlier sessions.} For a patient's later session (post-treatment, or follow-up), the patient's own earlier sessions served as donors in the pooled decoder, each session re-centred on its own mean and all of them together weighted like the calibration epochs. In the no-label variant, a decoder was trained on the earlier sessions alone, and the later session was re-centred on its calibration epochs without using their labels.

\paragraph{Co-adaptive recalibration.} As a descriptive reference, decoders were also refitted before every test trial on all earlier trials, labelled by their cues, as in co-adaptive calibration \citep{vidaurre2011}. This uses more labelled data as the session proceeds and is a different protocol from fixed calibration.

\paragraph{Deep transfer.} EEGNet \citep{lawhern2018} was trained with Euclidean alignment \citep{he2020}, random 2\,s crops and AdamW, pretrained for 60 epochs on donor data and fine-tuned for 40 epochs on the target's calibration data (implementation from braindecode \citep{schirrmeister2017}). Euclidean alignment requires the inverse square root of the mean covariance. Common-average-referenced data have a null direction, so directions with variance below $10^{-6}$ of the largest were dropped rather than inverted (Section~\ref{sec:pitfalls}).

\paragraph{Foundation model.} CBraMod \citep{wang2025cbramod}, pretrained on a large clinical EEG corpus, was used frozen. Epochs were resampled to 200\,Hz and scaled to units of 100\,\textmu V as the model expects; embeddings of 1\,s patches were averaged and classified by shrinkage linear discriminant analysis trained on the calibration epochs.

\subsection{LesionSim}
\label{sec:lesionsim-method}

LesionSim transforms healthy imagery epochs into stroke-like epochs by editing band envelopes while keeping phase. A band component $b(t)$ of a channel is split by the Hilbert transform into amplitude $A(t)$ and phase $\varphi(t)$, the amplitude is edited, and $b'(t) = A'(t)\cos\varphi(t)$ replaces $b(t)$. With the edit switched off, the input is reproduced exactly; unit tests enforce this identity. The rest amplitude $A_0$ of each channel is taken from the participant's own rest epochs. Three operators were implemented. The choice followed a preliminary comparison of S1 with H1 on development data, which found greater trial-to-trial variability of beta-band activity at C3 in stroke (Holm-corrected $p = 0.005$) and a trend toward reduced laterality. It also followed reports of more variable, more diffuse modulation in stroke EEG \citep{via2026} and of activity moving to the contralesional hemisphere with severity \citep{mansour2022}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{O1, weaker lesioned-side desynchronisation:} $A' = A_0 + \alpha\,(A - A_0)$ on ipsilesional channels in the mu band ($\alpha = 1$: unchanged).
  \item \emph{O2, contralesional shift:} a share $\beta$ of each ipsilesional channel's relative desynchronisation is added to its mirror channel.
  \item \emph{O4b, variability:} each trial's ipsilesional desynchronisation is scaled by a log-normal gain with log-standard-deviation $s$, in the mu and beta bands.
\end{itemize}
After editing, epochs were re-referenced to the common average, like real data. LesionSim was evaluated in three modes. In \emph{population mode}, settings were drawn per participant ($\alpha \sim U(0.6, 1)$, $\beta \sim U(0.2, 0.8)$, $s = 0.5$). In \emph{patient-matched mode}, $\alpha$ and $\beta$ were estimated from the target patient's calibration epochs as the ratio of the patient's to the healthy mean relative mu desynchronisation on FC3/C3/CP3, and its excess on FC4/C4/CP4. In \emph{learned mode}, 27 settings ($\alpha \in \{1, 0.6, 0.2\}$, $\beta \in \{0, 0.4, 0.8\}$, $s \in \{0, 0.5, 1\}$) were scored, and one was chosen honestly with four folds over patients (chosen on 12, scored on 4).

\subsection{SafeTransfer gate}

For each patient, every candidate decoder (own first, then transfer and their average) was scored by leave-one-pair-out cross-validation on the calibration pairs only. The gate selects the best candidate, with ties going to the patient's own decoder, but only if its calibration accuracy beats chance by the exact binomial test at $\alpha = 0.05$, Bonferroni-corrected over the candidates. Otherwise it abstains, and the system reports that more calibration is needed instead of giving possibly misleading feedback.

\subsection{Combination decoders}
\label{sec:combo-method}

\paragraph{Building blocks.} Each combination decoder is the re-centred tangent-space logistic regression above, assembled from four parts.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Features:} the mu/low-beta/high-beta band bank, on the 14-channel template or on a 28-channel central strip (the F, FC, C and CP rows and P3, Pz, P4; no frontal-pole, temporal or occipital channels).
  \item \emph{Donor groups:} the other patients' recordings of the same session and, in later sessions, the patient's own earlier sessions. Each group together weighs as much as the patient's labelled trials.
  \item \emph{Online re-centring:} before each test trial, the patient's re-centring mean is updated with every earlier test trial. This needs no labels.
  \item \emph{Co-adaptive updating:} before each test trial, the logistic regression is refitted with the cue labels of every earlier trial \citep{vidaurre2011}. In a cue-based training session these labels are known.
\end{itemize}
In the no-label decoder for returning patients, the logistic regression is trained on the earlier sessions only, and only the re-centring adapts.

\paragraph{Online model averaging.} The full system averages the predicted probabilities of ten of these decoders in later sessions, and six in the first session. Each decoder is weighted by its likelihood on the test trials decoded so far, which is Bayesian model averaging with the cue labels arriving after each decision. The members were fixed before the first development run: static and co-adaptive band-bank decoders on 14 and 28 channels, a variant with an empirical-Bayes weight prior and, in later sessions, the earlier-session stacks.

\paragraph{Development and confirmation.} Twenty-seven decoders, in two declared rounds, were compared on the 16 S3 development patients and on S4. S4's confirmatory tests were spent, so it counted as development data. Selection was done without hindsight, both leave-one-patient-out and across cohorts, and this phase is exploratory. Six hypotheses about the frozen decoders were then committed (Table~\ref{tab:prereg}). They were tested once on the eight S3 locked patients in all their sessions, with the 16 development patients as donors. Before that run, no combination decoder had been applied to these patients. A declared sensitivity analysis repeated the tests on the authors' ICA-cleaned recordings of the same patients.

\paragraph{Audits and external test.} A declared audit then checked the combination decoders for leakage and overfitting. First, S3's trials alternate between rest and imagery, so a decoder updated after every trial can use a trial's position in the sequence; the online decoders and the averaging weights were therefore rerun with updates only between (rest, imagery) pairs, so that every update sees a class-balanced set. Second, every patient's labels were shuffled within each session, donors included, and every decoder had to fall to chance. Third, in S4 the calibration was made strictly chronological: every trial up to the one that completes $k$ per class calibrates, so no test trial precedes a calibration trial. Fourth, unit tests check that each online decision uses only earlier trials. Fifth, patients possibly shared by S3 and S4 were identified from demographics and covariance fingerprints, and the tests repeated without them. Finally, three further hypotheses (H-E1 to H-E3, Table~\ref{tab:prereg}) were committed and tested once on all 49 usable S1 patients: the frozen 14-channel decoders, as S1 lacks the 28-channel strip, with pair-wise updates, as S1's hands alternate too.

\subsection{Exploratory sweep and artifact controls}
\label{sec:sweep-method}

\paragraph{Sweep.} On the 16 S3 development patients, the pooled decoder was varied one design choice at a time and in combinations: 14, 28 or all 59 channels; the 0.5--4.0\,s window or 0.5--5.5\,s; single bands, band banks and a nine-member ensemble; donor weights of 0.5--8 times the calibration; regularisation; the 8 nearest or best-fitting donors; per-domain stretching to unit dispersion \citep{rodrigues2019}; a linear support vector machine; mixing in synthetic calibration epochs; no labels; and online adaptation. The sweep was repeated on the same patients' post-treatment sessions. The gain of ``picking the best variant'' was estimated without hindsight by leave-one-patient-out selection.

\paragraph{Artifact controls.} Own and pooled decoders were also trained on channel groups that should carry little motor signal: frontal channels at 1--8\,Hz (eye movements), occipital or posterior channels at 8--30\,Hz (visual cortex) and temporal channels (muscle), each re-referenced to its own average. The raw EOG channels of S3 could not be regressed out: in most runs they were flat or far outside the physiological range. The decoders were therefore rerun on the dataset authors' own cleaned release (43 channels, average reference, independent component analysis). Whether the confirmed S3 result survives this cleaning was checked once on the locked test patients. This sensitivity analysis was declared in the log before it was run, and it could not add a confirmed result.

\subsection{Neurophysiological analyses}

For S3 (all 24 patients; descriptive, with fixed methods) and the healthy cohort, the relative desynchronisation (ERD) of each channel was computed as $(\bar A_{\mathrm{MI}} - \bar A_{\mathrm{rest}}) / \bar A_{\mathrm{rest}}$ from Hilbert envelopes in the mu and beta bands. To explain why some donors help, each patient's imagery-versus-rest direction in the re-centred tangent space (the difference of class means, unit length) was compared by cosine similarity with the pooled direction of the healthy donors and with that of the other patients. Its split-half reliability (odd versus even pairs) gives the ceiling. Finally, the own decoder's blocked cross-validation accuracy was compared across the three S3 time points.

\subsection{Online implementation}
\label{sec:online-method}

The decoders were packaged for streaming use. A replay process publishes a recording's raw 64-channel EEG and cue markers as Lab Streaming Layer streams \citep{kothe2025}, paced in real time, as an amplifier and a stimulus program would. A receiver applies a causal 1--40\,Hz filter, cuts each trial's window as soon as it has arrived, harmonises it, calibrates on the first five rest--imagery pairs and then decodes every later epoch with the own and the pooled decoder. The decoders are the same code as in the offline evaluation; unit tests check that they give identical outputs.
