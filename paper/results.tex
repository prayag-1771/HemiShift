\subsection{The published S1 baselines are not reproducible, and the S1 signal is weak}
\label{sec:pitfalls}

Porting the authors' released analysis code to the authors' raw data gave 50.56\pct{} (CSP with linear discriminant analysis; 95\pct{} CI 47.8--53.4) and 49.29\pct{} (filter-bank CSP with a support vector machine; 46.9--51.7). The published values are 55.57\pct{} and 57.57\pct{} \citep{liu2024}. The cleaned recordings gave the same (51.86\pct{} and 49.17\pct{}), which agrees with the 49.46--51.82\pct{} reported by others on this cohort \citep{via2026}. A label-free audit of the 34 development patients explains part of the difficulty:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Every patient had 1--9 technically faulty channels, and 27 had at least one faulty motor channel.
  \item A rhythm peak above the aperiodic background was present in only 20 patients.
  \item The classic lateralised power difference between C3 and C4 was significant in only 2 (mu) and 4 (beta) patients, against 1.7 expected by chance.
\end{itemize}

Three further pitfalls inflated results during development and are reported so that others can avoid them.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Per-patient tuning without nesting.} Choosing each patient's best window and band in hindsight gave 72.7\pct{}; the same choice made honestly by nested cross-validation gave 59.2\pct{}, against 57.4\pct{} for a fixed choice (Figure~\ref{fig:beijing}a). The 13.5-point gap is selection luck on 40 trials \citep{varoquaux2018}.
  \item \textbf{Transductive drift correction.} Re-centring each block of test trials on its own mean improved accuracy by 5.1 points ($p = 0.016$), but the gain vanished when only past trials could be used, as in a real session. It came from looking ahead.
  \item \textbf{Euclidean alignment on common-average data.} A plain inverse square root amplified the null direction of common-average data about $10^6$-fold. This injected noise into every aligned input and pushed edited copies to values near $10^7$. Before the fix, healthy-pretrained EEGNet appeared to harm 22 of 34 patients significantly ($p = 0.034$). After dropping null directions, held-out healthy accuracy rose from 63.0\pct{} to 67.7\pct{}, and the apparent negative transfer disappeared (15 helped, 16 harmed, $p = 0.26$).
  \item \textbf{Floating-point ties in rank tests.} Per-patient accuracies are fractions of a few dozen trials. Equal differences, such as four patients each gaining one of 70 test trials, came out of floating point a few units in the last place apart, and the Wilcoxon test ranked them apart instead of as ties. This turned the correct $p = 0.047$ of the S3 locked test into the first-logged 0.035. All reported $p$-values are recomputed with differences rounded to $10^{-9}$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Left- versus right-hand imagery in acute stroke (S1)}

Within patients (blocked cross-validation, 34 development patients), the own decoder reached 57.7\pct{}, with 8 patients at or above the 65\pct{} chance threshold. None of the following beat it:
\begin{itemize}
  \item EEGNet from scratch (57.4\pct{}) or pretrained on healthy data and fine-tuned (55.7\pct{});
  \item the frozen foundation-model probe (52.1--52.9\pct{});
  \item slow cortical potentials (57.2\pct{});
  \item phase-lagged coherence (46.7--52.4\pct{});
  \item individually tuned frequency bands (53.4\pct{});
  \item eleven variants of the classical decoder (regularisation, discriminant analysis, minimum distance to mean, CSP, channel subsets, time crops, outlier removal).
\end{itemize}
LesionSim did not improve healthy pretraining either (54.5--56.2\pct{} for single operators and their combination, against 56.5\pct{} for healthy data alone). Strikingly, pretraining on the other development patients' real recordings gave no gain either (56.0\pct{}).

The window ensemble was the only decoder that improved significantly on the own decoder during development (uncorrected): 61.5\pct{} versus 57.4\pct{} on the same 39 trials. It helped 20 patients and harmed 9, raised the number of patients above chance from 8 to 13, and gave $p = 0.012$ (Holm-corrected over 11 variants: 0.13). The gain needed the post-imagery window: without it the ensemble scored 58.2\pct{}. On the 15 locked test patients the ensemble improved by a similar amount (+3.6 points), but the pre-registered one-sided test gave $p = 0.083$ (Table~\ref{tab:prereg}, Figure~\ref{fig:beijing}b).

Who can use a left/right BCI could be predicted from calibration data, but not from clinical scores. Under chronological calibration on the first 20 trials, the gate passed 8 of 34 development patients. These scored 72.6\pct{} on their later trials, against 51.8\pct{} for the 26 it abstained on ($p < 0.001$). Calibration and later accuracy correlated ($\rho = 0.48$, $p = 0.004$), while later accuracy was unrelated to stroke severity (NIHSS, $\rho = -0.05$) or time since stroke ($\rho = -0.13$). On the locked test patients the gate passed only one patient (78.9\pct{} on later trials), so the pre-registered comparison was not evaluable (Figure~\ref{fig:beijing}c).

\subsection{Few-trial calibration of affected-hand imagery versus rest (S3)}
\label{sec:s3}

Task~B is far easier: the own decoder reached 81.7\pct{} with five calibration pairs and 84.7\pct{} with ten (Figure~\ref{fig:strategies}).
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Deep transfer.} Every fine-tuned variant trailed the own decoder at both calibration sizes, by 4.5--12.2 points: healthy pretraining 76.6\pct{} and 76.3\pct{}, adding a LesionSim copy 76.3\pct{} and 76.5\pct{}, adding a generic-augmentation copy 74.5\pct{} and 72.5\pct{}, adding the other patients 77.2\pct{} and 76.3\pct{}. The deep models barely profited from twice as much calibration data.
  \item \textbf{Frozen foundation model.} The CBraMod probe trailed by 14--24 points (58.2--68.2\pct{}).
  \item \textbf{Riemannian pooling.} Pooling healthy donors into the re-centred Riemannian decoder did not help (78.9\pct{} and 84.3\pct{}), nor did LesionSim donors in population mode (79.7\pct{} and 84.2\pct{}) or patient-matched mode (78.6\pct{} and 83.5\pct{}).
\end{itemize}

Pooling the other development patients' recordings was the only strategy that improved on the own decoder. With five calibration pairs it reached 84.0\pct{}; median gain +1.4 points, $p = 0.003$, Holm-corrected $p = 0.012$; 11 of 16 patients helped and none harmed by 2 points or more. With ten pairs it reached 86.1\pct{} (+0.8 points, $p = 0.20$). The pre-registered test on the eight locked patients confirmed the five-pair result: 77.1\pct{} to 81.4\pct{}, one-sided $p = 0.047$, with 7 patients helped, 1 harmed, and all 8 above chance afterwards (Figure~\ref{fig:pooling}). The replication on S4 is reported in Section~\ref{sec:s4}. Pooled decoding with five calibration pairs (81.4\pct{}) matched the own decoder with ten (79.5\pct{}), halving the calibration needed.

\subsection{What else improves the pooled decoder: an exploratory sweep (S3 development)}
\label{sec:sweep}

Forty variants of the pooled decoder were run on the 16 development patients, in their first and their second session (Figure~\ref{fig:robustness}a). No fixed-calibration variant survived Holm correction over the 31 comparisons of the first session.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Splitting the band helped most consistently.} A bank of theta, mu, low-beta and high-beta covariances added 3.0 and 3.7 points over the pooled decoder in the two sessions (second session $p = 0.004$; 13 helped, 2 harmed). Mu alone was worse ($-4.7$ points, Holm $p = 0.045$).
  \item \textbf{More channels helped somewhat.} 28 or 59 channels added 1.2--2.7 points, and up to 4.8 with a band bank, mostly in the second session. Part of this came from channels outside the motor strip (Section~\ref{sec:artifacts}).
  \item \textbf{Other choices did not help.} A longer window, donor weighting, choosing the nearest or best-fitting donors, stretching, a support vector machine, a nine-member ensemble and synthetic calibration epochs changed the mean by between $-1.9$ and $+0.8$ points; stretching cost the most.
  \item \textbf{Honest selection.} Picking the best variant without hindsight gained 1.8 points in the first session ($p = 0.22$) and 3.0 in the second ($p = 0.020$).
  \item \textbf{Protocol matters most.} The only Holm-significant gain came from refitting the decoder after every trial with its cue label (co-adaptive recalibration): 88.6\pct{} against 84.0\pct{} (Holm $p = 0.012$). Once labels kept arriving, pooling added nothing (own decoder, recalibrated: 88.5\pct{}).
\end{itemize}
The band bank without its theta band was carried forward as S4's hypothesis H-R2, for the reason given next.

\subsection{Imagery versus rest is not purely motor}
\label{sec:artifacts}

Channels far from motor cortex also separated imagery from rest in S3. On the development patients, the own decoder with five pairs gave:
\begin{itemize}
  \item frontal channels at 1--8\,Hz, where eye movements and blinks dominate: 77.3\pct{};
  \item occipital channels at 8--30\,Hz: 74.3\pct{};
  \item temporal channels: 66.1\pct{};
  \item frontal channels at 8--30\,Hz: only 60.3\pct{};
  \item the strict motor strip (FC, C and CP rows): 81.1\pct{}, close to the 14-channel template (81.7\pct{}).
\end{itemize}
On the dataset authors' ICA-cleaned release, the eye control fell to 55.2\pct{}, while motor decoding stayed at 83.6--84.1\pct{} (Figure~\ref{fig:robustness}b). Cleaning raised the own decoder enough that the single-band pooled decoder no longer beat it (84.0\pct{} vs 83.6\pct{}). The band-bank pooled decoder still did (86.0\pct{} vs 84.1\pct{}, $p = 0.020$; 9 helped, 1 harmed).

The declared sensitivity analysis on the eight locked patients gave the same picture (Figure~\ref{fig:robustness}c). On their cleaned recordings, single-band pooling gained 1.6 points over the own decoder ($p = 0.29$) and band-bank pooling 3.5 points ($p = 0.031$; 4.8 points with ten pairs, $p = 0.031$). Posterior channels still separated the classes after cleaning (72.2\pct{}), so part of what an imagery-versus-rest decoder detects in this paradigm is probably visual or attentional engagement, not motor imagery alone.

\subsection{Replication on a new cohort, and returning patients (S4)}
\label{sec:s4}

All four pre-registered hypotheses held on S4 (Table~\ref{tab:prereg}, Figure~\ref{fig:replication}).
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Pooling replicated.} Pooling the other 26 patients raised accuracy with five calibration trials per class from 69.1\pct{} to 70.8\pct{} ($p = 0.019$; 14 helped, 6 harmed). The gain was smaller than on S3, at a lower level of accuracy, for a different limb and with randomised rather than paired trials.
  \item \textbf{The band bank added more.} It reached 73.1\pct{}: 2.3 points over the pooled decoder (Holm $p = 0.021$) and 4.0 over the patient's own.
  \item \textbf{Earlier sessions helped.} In the post-treatment and follow-up sessions, the patient's own earlier sessions raised accuracy by 3.2 points on average (Holm $p = 0.0045$): 5.4 points at follow-up (17 helped, 2 harmed) and 1.5 after treatment. S3 development patients showed the same pattern, with 4.7 points at follow-up (9 helped, 1 harmed; Holm $p = 0.031$).
  \item \textbf{No new labels were needed.} The earlier sessions' decoder, without labels from the later session, reached 69.7\pct{} against 67.4\pct{} for the own decoder with five labelled pairs. That is non-inferior at the 2-point margin (Holm $p = 0.021$). On S3 development patients at follow-up it reached 84.5\pct{} against 77.9\pct{}.
\end{itemize}
Descriptively:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Co-adaptive recalibration again gave the largest gain: 75.2\pct{} for the own decoder, +6.1 points.
  \item A decoder from the other patients alone, with no labels, reached 68.5\pct{} ($-0.7$ points).
  \item A 59-channel band bank reached 77.9\pct{}.
  \item Non-motor channels carried less information than in S3: frontal 1--8\,Hz 64.9\pct{}, occipital 63.7\pct{}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Combining the methods: about 12 points on never-used patients}
\label{sec:combo}

\paragraph{Development.} The gains added up (Figure~\ref{fig:combo}a,b). With five calibration pairs, in both development cohorts:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{First session.} Other patients and the band bank lifted the own decoder by 4--5 points. Online re-centring, which needs no labels, added 1--2 points. Co-adaptive updating with the cue labels and model averaging brought the total to 9.3 points on S3 (81.7\pct{} $\rightarrow$ 91.0\pct{}) and 11.2 points on S4 (69.1\pct{} $\rightarrow$ 80.3\pct{}; 26 of 27 patients helped).
  \item \textbf{Returning patients.} The earlier sessions, the other patients and the 28-channel band bank together gained 8.0 points on both cohorts without labels after calibration. With online re-centring they gained 8.8 and 9.8 points, and with co-adaptive updating and model averaging 10.3 and 11.6 points.
  \item \textbf{Beyond co-adaptive recalibration.} Model averaging beat co-adaptive recalibration of the patient's own decoder, the established technique, by 3.7 to 6.1 points in every cohort and setting.
  \item \textbf{Choices transferred across cohorts.} The best decoder picked on S3 scored 11.2 (first session) and 11.6 points (returning) above the own decoder on S4. The best decoders picked on S4 scored 9.3 and 10.3 points above it on S3.
  \item \textbf{What did not help.} An empirical-Bayes prior on the decoder weights, used instead of pooling donor trials, was worse than pooling in every setting. Sub-window augmentation and unsupervised bias adaptation did not help. A third declared round tried forgetting old trials, a fixed donor weight, donors from every session, early and late time-window experts and a larger model average. None beat model averaging by more than 1 point in any cohort or setting, and choices made on one cohort added 0.0--0.9 points on the other. The combination has plateaued at about 90\pct{} on S3 and 80\pct{} on S4.
  \item \textbf{Artifacts.} On the ICA-cleaned development recordings, the co-adaptive stacks kept most of their gain: 6.2 points in the first session, and 7.4 and 8.6 points in later sessions. So did the no-label stack for returning patients, at 7.3 points. The static no-label decoders kept about half.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Confirmation on the locked patients.} All six pre-registered hypotheses held (Table~\ref{tab:prereg}, Figure~\ref{fig:combo}c,d).
\begin{itemize}
  \item With co-adaptive updating and model averaging, accuracy rose from 77.1\pct{} to 88.7\pct{} in the first session and from 75.9\pct{} to 88.1\pct{} in later sessions. Every patient improved in both settings.
  \item In the first session the stack beat co-adaptive recalibration of the own decoder by 5.2 points (7 of 8 patients). The audit below lowered this margin to 3.0 points.
  \item Without labels after calibration, returning patients reached 87.7\pct{} (+11.9 points, 8 of 8) and first-session patients 85.8\pct{} (+8.7, 7 of 8).
  \item With no labels at all in the later session, the earlier sessions' decoder with online re-centring reached 84.2\pct{}, 8.4 points above the own decoder with five labelled pairs (8 of 8).
  \item With ten calibration pairs, the full stack gained 7.7 points in the first session and 12.5 in later sessions, again for every patient.
\end{itemize}
The declared sensitivity analysis on the authors' ICA-cleaned recordings of the same patients gave the same verdicts for all six hypotheses (one-sided $p = 0.004$--$0.039$). The full stack gained 10.6 points in the first session (7 of 8 patients) and 12.8 in later sessions (8 of 8). It beat co-adaptive recalibration of the own decoder by 4.9 points. The no-label stacks gained 12.4 (returning, no labels after calibration), 9.5 (no labels at all) and 6.9 points (first session).

\paragraph{Audits.}\label{sec:audit} The declared leakage and overfitting audit left every verdict standing.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Trial order.} S3's trials alternate between rest and imagery, so an update after every trial can carry a trial's position in the sequence. With updates only between (rest, imagery) pairs, the stack gained 12.0 points in the first session and 12.0 in later sessions (both $p = 0.0039$), and all six hypotheses held, on raw and on cleaned recordings (Table~\ref{tab:prereg}). Per-trial updating had handicapped co-adaptive recalibration of the own decoder: just before each imagery trial it had learned the label of the paired rest trial. Updated pair-wise, it reached 86.0\pct{} instead of 83.5\pct{}. The stack's honest margin over it is therefore 3.0 points (7 of 8 patients, $p = 0.047$), and 2.6 points on the cleaned recordings ($p = 0.016$).
  \item \textbf{Shuffled labels.} With every patient's labels shuffled within each session, donors included, all 27 decoders scored 47--52\pct{} on the S3 development patients and on S4. Model averaging scored 48.5\pct{} and 49.1\pct{}, against 91.0\pct{} and 80.3\pct{} with the true labels. The smallest $p$-value against 50\pct{}, 0.007 (single-band pooling, S4 later sessions, 52.3\pct{}), came from the highest of 27 decoders in one of 216 decoder-by-setting checks, which chance explains.
  \item \textbf{Chronological calibration.} With five trials per class, the first trials of each class calibrate, so in S4's randomised order some test trials precede the last calibration trial. With strictly chronological calibration, model averaging scored 80.6\pct{} and 79.1\pct{} in S4's first and later sessions (80.3\pct{} and 79.1\pct{} before), 13.6 and 11.9 points above the own decoder (27 of 27 and 22 of 23 patients helped). Only the own decoder lost accuracy, because its calibration set became class-imbalanced.
  \item \textbf{Shared patients.} Two locked S3 patients may also appear in S4: they match an S4 patient in age, sex and lesion side, and covariance fingerprints could not settle it. Without them, all six hypotheses still held ($p = 0.016$ each, 6 of 6 patients helped).
  \item \textbf{Causality.} Unit tests confirm that each online decision depends only on earlier trials and labels, and, with pair-wise updates, only on complete earlier pairs.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{A second hospital.} Applied unchanged to all 49 usable S1 patients, with a different hospital, amplifier, stage (acute) and task (affected- versus unaffected-hand imagery), the stack with pair-wise updates beat the own decoder with five calibration trials per hand: 52.5\pct{} $\rightarrow$ 56.9\pct{} (+4.4 points; 31 helped, 14 harmed; $p = 0.0027$; H-E1). It did not beat co-adaptive recalibration of the own decoder, which reached 56.2\pct{} on its own (+0.7, $p = 0.17$; H-E2), and this stopped the fixed sequence. The no-label stack scored 57.1\pct{} (+4.6 points, descriptive $p = 0.0009$; H-E3, not tested). With ten trials per hand the gain shrank to 1.6 points ($p = 0.14$). On this weak left--right signal, the stack helped with very short calibration, and co-adaptive recalibration alone delivered most of that help.

\subsection{LesionSim does not turn healthy donors into useful ones}
\label{sec:lesionsim}

Real patients were better donors than healthy participants by 5.1 points (84.0\pct{} vs 78.9\pct{} with five pairs). If LesionSim captured what distinguishes stroke data, its output should fall between the two. It did not.
\begin{itemize}
  \item All 27 learned settings scored between 78.5\pct{} and 80.0\pct{} (Figure~\ref{fig:lesionsim}).
  \item Chosen honestly across patients, the best setting gave 78.8\pct{}, no better than unmodified healthy donors (median $-0.7$ points, $p = 0.86$). It closed none of the gap; even the best setting in hindsight closed only 21\pct{} of it.
  \item The honestly chosen setting differed in every fold.
  \item In patient-matched mode, the estimated attenuation and shift mostly sat at their bounds: $\alpha = 1$ and $\beta = 1$ in the median. The chronic patients' ipsilesional mu desynchronisation was usually not weaker than the healthy participants' ($-13\pct{}$ ipsilateral and $-9\pct{}$ contralateral on average), contrary to the premise of operator O1.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{SafeTransfer protects against harmful transfer}
\label{sec:gate}

When the transferred decoder was the LesionSim-pretrained EEGNet, always transferring harmed 9 of 16 development patients with five calibration pairs and 11 of 16 with ten. The gate harmed 2 and 1 (exact McNemar tests, $p = 0.008$ and $p = 0.001$; Figure~\ref{fig:gate}). It also raised mean accuracy over always transferring, by 4.2 points with five pairs (95\pct{} CI 0.0 to 8.6, $p = 0.032$) and by 8.1 points with ten (1.7 to 13.3, $p = 0.001$). The gate mostly kept the patient's own decoder. It did not beat always using the own decoder (80.6\pct{} vs 81.7\pct{} and 84.4\pct{} vs 84.7\pct{}). Its value is safety: when it is not known whether a transferred model helps a given patient, the gate decides from calibration data alone. With five pairs it abstained for 4 patients, and the remaining 12 reached 83.5\pct{}.

\subsection{Real-time operation}
\label{sec:online}

Streaming each S3 locked test patient's raw recording through the online pipeline reproduced the offline evaluation. With five calibration pairs, the own decoder averaged 77.1\pct{} online and offline, and the pooled decoder 82.5\pct{} online against 81.4\pct{} offline; per-patient differences came only from the causal filter. Processing took a median of 12.6\,ms per epoch in this streaming check. Over real Lab Streaming Layer streams, with the machine under full load, it took 21--23\,ms (maximum 54\,ms), and the final accuracies were identical to the streaming check. A decision is therefore available about 4\,s after each cue, at the end of the imagery window.

\section{Discussion}

Across three stroke cohorts and four ways of borrowing data, only real stroke recordings helped a patient with few calibration trials: other patients' recordings, and the patient's own earlier sessions. Pooling other patients into a re-centred Riemannian decoder lifted accuracy with five rest--imagery pairs on never-seen patients, to what the patient's own decoder needed ten pairs for, and the gain replicated in a new cohort with a different limb and trial design. Splitting the decoder's band into mu, low-beta and high-beta covariances added further accuracy in that replication. For a returning patient, the earlier sessions helped most at follow-up, months later, and without any new labels they matched five labelled calibration pairs. Healthy recordings did not help in any form: as pretraining for a deep network, as donors for the Riemannian decoder, transformed by a physiology-based simulator in six variants, or distilled into a foundation model.

The sources of real data, and the ways of adapting to them, add up. Stacked in one decoder with online re-centring, co-adaptive updating and online model averaging, they raised accuracy on eight never-used patients by about 12 points, from 76--77\pct{} to 88--89\pct{}, in the first session and when the patients returned, and every patient improved. Co-adaptive recalibration on its own is an established technique \citep{vidaurre2011}. The pre-registered comparison with it isolates what the transfer methods add on top of it: about 3 points in the first session once the updates no longer carried the trial order, and nothing significant at a second hospital, where the left--right signal was weak. For clinical use, two results matter more than the headline. Without any labels after calibration, the stack still gained about 12 points for returning patients, so a self-paced session that cannot rely on cues would benefit too. With no labels at all in a returning session, the patient's earlier sessions and online re-centring beat a fresh five-pair calibration by 8 points, so from the second session on, labelled calibration can be skipped.

Why do real patients help when healthy participants do not? Re-centring removes each domain's average covariance, so what remains to transfer is the direction that separates imagery from rest. Healthy and stroke brains, recorded with different equipment and paradigms, differ in that direction more than stroke patients differ from each other (Section~\ref{sec:neuro}). LesionSim edits a few envelope statistics of healthy data, such as lesioned-side attenuation, contralesional shift and trial-to-trial variability. Even with learned settings this moved the healthy donors less than a quarter of the way toward real patients, and the patients it was meant to imitate did not show the assumed weaker lesioned-side desynchronisation. The same argument explains why a patient's own earlier sessions transfer so well: the direction is the patient's own, and decodability was stable across the study period (Section~\ref{sec:neuro}). The practical conclusion is to pool real patient data across hospitals rather than synthesise it, to keep each patient's recordings from session to session, and to share stroke recordings in harmonised form.

Imagery-versus-rest paradigms invite non-motor cues. In S3, eye movements and posterior alpha alone separated imagery from rest well above chance. The motor decoders did not depend on them: frontal channels in the motor band were weak, the strict motor strip performed like the full template, and decoding held after ICA cleaning removed the eye signal. Cleaning did, however, remove most of the single-band pooled decoder's advantage, while the band-bank pooled decoder kept it, in development and on the locked test patients. We therefore recommend the band-bank version, and we suggest that imagery-versus-rest studies report non-motor channel controls.

The results also bound what can be expected from left- versus right-hand imagery early after stroke with a portable 29-channel system and 40 trials. Most patients did not reach chance-adjusted significance with any decoder, and pretraining on the other patients did not change that. Calibration data identified who could, while clinical scores could not. A short screening session could therefore decide whether imagery-based feedback is worth offering, and the gate's abstention is the honest output for the rest.

Several evaluation pitfalls produced effects as large as the method differences studied here: hindsight selection (+13.5 points), transductive re-centring (+5 points) and a numerical failure of Euclidean alignment that manufactured negative transfer. A subtler one was floating-point ties in a rank test, which made a confirmatory $p$-value look smaller than it is (0.035 instead of 0.047). Locked test sets, chronological calibration, pre-registered single-shot confirmation and recomputing every statistic from the saved per-patient results kept them out of the conclusions.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The locked test sets are small (8 and 15 patients). The S3 pooling confirmation rests on a one-sided test at $p = 0.047$, close to the threshold. The S1 locked set was used for two pre-registered analyses and the S3 locked set for three, each with hypotheses committed beforehand. The combination study was confirmed on those eight S3 patients, all from one site, and its development used S4 after S4's own confirmatory tests. At a second hospital (S1) it beat the own decoder but not co-adaptive recalibration.
  \item The pre-registered combination runs updated the decoders after every trial. In S3's alternating sequence this carried the trial order and handicapped co-adaptive recalibration of the own decoder. With pair-wise updates the stack's margin over it is 3.0 points, not 5.2.
  \item The largest gains need the cue label of every trial during the session. A cue-based rehabilitation session has these labels, but a self-paced one does not. There, the no-label stacks apply. Some of their gains were smaller on cleaned development recordings, although all held on the cleaned locked recordings.
  \item The replication cohort comes from the same institution and amplifier family as S3, and some patients may appear in both. Without the two locked S3 patients who may also be in S4, all six combination hypotheses still held. It replicates the method on new recordings, a new limb and a new trial design, but not at a new site.
  \item On ICA-cleaned recordings, the confirmed single-band pooling gain shrank to 1.6 points on the locked patients, while the band-bank variant kept its gain. The S4 confirmations used raw recordings, as pre-registered.
  \item Imagery-versus-rest decoding in these paradigms partly reflects non-motor engagement (eye movements, posterior alpha). Co-adaptive recalibration is an established technique; we claim only what the transfer methods add to it.
  \item All analyses are offline. The real-time system replays recordings rather than closing the loop with patients, and no clinical outcome was measured.
  \item Device and site are confounded with health status: healthy donors were recorded on a different system and paradigm from either stroke cohort. Part of the healthy-donor gap may therefore be technical rather than neurological. Same-device healthy controls (HEFMI-ICH) carried too little imagery signal to separate the two.
  \item LesionSim covers one family of envelope operators. A learned generative translation from healthy to stroke EEG was not attempted.
  \item A third left/right stroke cohort \citep{mathi2025} requires an access password that had not arrived at the time of writing.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

For motor-imagery BCIs after stroke, data from other stroke patients and from the patient's own earlier sessions shorten calibration, while healthy data, simulated stroke data and frozen foundation-model features do not. A re-centred Riemannian decoder pooled across patients improved few-trial decoding on never-seen patients and in a pre-registered replication. A mu/beta band bank strengthened it and kept it robust to artifact cleaning, a returning patient's earlier sessions removed the need for labelled calibration, and a calibration-only gate prevented harmful transfer. Stacked with online re-centring, co-adaptive updating and online model averaging, these sources raised accuracy on never-used patients by about 12 points, to 88--89\pct{}, in their first and later sessions. Building such systems will depend on shared, harmonised patient data and on keeping each patient's history, rather than on simulating patients from healthy brains.
